## Historical Context

The 1970s adventure aesthetic wasn't manufactured — it accumulated. Dust on a VW Westfalia dashboard. A dog-eared Rand McNally atlas. The golden-hour palette of Kodachrome film stock that made every roadside pull-off look like revelation. This was the era when National Geographic still felt dangerous, when their photographers disappeared into jungles for months and came back with images that smelled like humidity and diesel. What made it stick wasn't nostalgia — it was material honesty. Canvas, leather, oxidized metal. Typography that referenced hand-painted trail signs and national park signage. The color language pulled from actual terrain: burnt sienna from canyon walls, avocado from lichen-covered rock, mustard from late-afternoon light hitting a tent. Nothing was optimized for screens because screens didn't matter yet. The VW van became the icon not because of marketing but because of limitation. You couldn't carry much. You slowed down. The vehicle itself enforced a philosophy — go further by going slower. That constraint produced an entire visual culture: patch-covered denim, hand-drawn maps, sunset gradient everything. It communicated that adventure meant friction, not frictionlessness.

## When to Use

Reach for this when the brand needs to feel lived-in rather than launched. Adventure travel blogs that want warmth over polish. Outdoor brands targeting the heritage crowd — people who care about waxed canvas and campfire coffee. Van life content that rejects the Instagram-clean aesthetic. Retro tourism campaigns where the destination matters less than the feeling of getting there. It falls apart fast on anything corporate or tech-forward. Keep it away from minimalism.

## Design Principles

- Earn your texture — every grain, every paper fold, every worn edge should reference a real material. Fake distress is worse than no distress.
- Color from geography, not from swatches. Pull palette from landscapes: ochre, forest shadow, faded denim, sun-bleached canvas. If it couldn't exist in nature, it doesn't belong.
- Typography should feel carved or stamped, never rendered. Reference park signage, trail markers, hand-lettered van murals. Geometric sans-serifs with rounded terminals. Slab serifs with weight.
- Embrace the analog grid — slightly loose, slightly imperfect. Overlapping elements, rotated photos, torn-edge masks. The layout should feel pinned to a corkboard, not snapped to a pixel grid.
- Warm light as a design element, not an afterthought. Golden hour isn't a filter — it's a commitment to a specific emotional temperature that runs through every component.

## Components

- **Primary Button:** Subtly rounded (0.5rem) shape. Accent color fill. Hover: 8% darken + subtle lift shadow. Active: -1px translate tactile press. Font weight 600. No outer glows.
- **Secondary / Ghost Button:** Outline variant. 1.5px border in muted color. Text in primary color. Hover: subtle background fill.
- **Cards:** Subtly rounded (0.5rem) corners. Surface background. Subtle shadow (0 2px 12px rgba(0,0,0,0.06)). 1px border stroke.
- **Inputs:** Label above input. 1px border stroke. Focus ring: 2px accent color offset 2px. Error text below in semantic red. No floating labels.
- **Navigation:** Primary surface background. Active item: accent color indicator. Font weight 500 when active.
- **Skeletons:** Shimmer animation matching component dimensions. No circular spinners.
- **Empty States:** Icon-based composition with descriptive text and action button.

## Do's and Don'ts

- No emojis in UI — use icon system only (Lucide, Heroicons)
- No pure black (#000000) — use off-black or charcoal variants
- No oversaturated accent colors (saturation cap: 80%)
- No 3-column equal-width feature layouts — use zig-zag or asymmetric grid
- No `h-screen` — use `min-h-[100dvh]`
- No AI copywriting clichés: "Elevate", "Seamless", "Unleash", "Next-Gen"
- No broken external image links — use picsum.photos or inline SVG
- No generic lorem ipsum in demos
