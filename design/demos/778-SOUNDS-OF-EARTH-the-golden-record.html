The mission

How a record leaves the solar system

Three steps between a studio in Ithaca and the space between the stars.

01

Capture

A committee led by Carl Sagan curates the sounds, images, and greetings that best tell the story of Earth.

02

Etch

The collection is pressed into gold-plated copper, sealed in an aluminum cover with a stylus and playback instructions.

03

Voyage

Twin Voyager probes carry the records past Jupiter, Saturn, and beyond the heliosphere into interstellar space.

Questions from mission control

Everything people ask before pressing play.

A 12-inch gold-plated copper disc placed aboard both Voyager spacecraft in 1977. It holds music, greetings, natural sounds, and encoded images describing life and culture on Earth, intended for any intelligence that might one day find it.

Gold does not corrode or react with other elements. A gold-plated copper disc can survive the radiation and vacuum of deep space for hundreds of millions of years with its grooves intact.

Estimates suggest the records will remain playable for at least a billion years, far longer than any other human artifact. They will likely outlast the pyramids, and possibly Earth itself.

Yes. Every plan includes the complete audio contents of the record, transferred from the original master tapes. Voyager and Golden Record plans add the lossless remaster and the decoded image gallery.

Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space in 2012 and is now more than 24 billion kilometers from the Sun, the most distant human-made object ever. It will drift past another star in about 40,000 years.
